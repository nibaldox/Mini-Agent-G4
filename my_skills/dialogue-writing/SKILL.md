# Dialogue Writing Skill

You are a dialogue specialist. Craft conversations that feel real, reveal character, and drive story:

## Fundamentals

### What Dialogue Does
- Reveals character (speech patterns, education, region)
- Advances plot (information transfer, conflict)
- Creates subtext (what characters mean vs say)
- Establishes relationships (power dynamics, history)
- Controls pacing (slow vs fast scenes)

### What Dialogue Should NOT Do
- Info-dump exposition
- Greeting/farewell filler ("Hello, how are you?")
- Explain what audience can see
- Sound unnatural for the character

## Character Voice

### Building Distinct Voices
- **Vocabulary**: Education, profession, era
- **Sentence length**: Nervous characters ramble
- **Rhythm**: Some people speak in fragments
- **Patterns**: Filler words, habitual phrases
- **Subculture**: slang, references, ways of speaking

### Example - Same Information, Different Voices

**Formal Character:**
"The documentation suggests we proceed with Option A."

**Casual Character:**
"Yeah so the docs say we should go with A, I think."

**Eloquent Character:**
"As the documentation indicates, Option A appears most prudent."

**Technical Character:**
"According to the spec, A is the recommended approach."

## Subtext

### Layers of Meaning
- Surface: What characters literally say
- Subtext: What they mean
- Silence: What they don't say

### Creating Subtext
- Character wants something but can't say it directly
- History between characters affects what's said
- Conflict creates tension in what's NOT said
- Physical actions reveal true feelings

### Example
```
Surface: "Nice weather we're having."
Subtext: "I want to break up with you but can't find the words."
Silence: Neither mentions the fight they had last night.
```

## Conversation Structure

### Opening
- Enter late, leave early (don't capture full conversation)
- Start with conflict or hook
- "Why are we having this conversation NOW?"

### Middle
- Escalate tension
- New information shifts dynamics
- Character makes choice or reveals something

### End
- Scene ends on a turn (something changes)
- Point to next scene
- Exit line that resonates

## Pacing

### Short Exchanges
- Quick back-and-forth for tension
- Fragmentary for urgency
- Cut common phrases

### Long Exchanges
- More detail when it's important
- Build for emotional moments
- Slow down for significant reveals

### Silence
- Pause marks: "...", "Silence stretched."
- Characters not speaking says something
- White space gives reader time to feel

## Format

### Punctuation
- Speaker first, then dialogue
- New line for new speaker
- Action on same line or separate
- Parentheticals for delivery, not explanation

### Examples
```
"Okay." Sarah grabbed her keys.

"Are you sure about this?" Marcus didn't look up from his screen.

"I have to go." (firm)

"Wait—" But the door was already closing.
```

## Common Issues

### On-the-Nose Dialogue
- Characters say exactly what they feel
- **Fix**: What would they really say to hide their feelings?

### Exposition Dialogue
- Characters explain things they wouldn't naturally explain
- **Fix**: Find a reason they need to say it (teaching, justifying)

### Same-Ore Voice
- All characters sound the same
- **Fix**: Give each character a speech pattern trait

### Overwritten
- Too many tags and beats
- **Fix**: One clear tag per line, reader will visualize

## Dialogue Tags

### When to Use
- First mention of who is speaking
- When speaker changes
- When action interrupts

### Tags to Avoid
- "said" alternatives for emotion (screamed, whispered)
- Too many adverbs
- Flowery or unusual tags

### Better Alternatives
- Action beats instead of tags
- Silence as a beat
- Let reader track speakers visually

## Practice

When writing dialogue:
1. Read aloud - would you say this?
2. Cover speaker names - do they sound different?
3. Ask what the scene is really about
4. Cut every line that doesn't earn its place
